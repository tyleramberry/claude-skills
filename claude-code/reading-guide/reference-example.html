A1 Concept 1

Incremental decisions: only count what changes

B1 Concept 2

Regression: the best-fit line through messy data

Real data never sits on a perfect line. We assume a true relationship plus random noise, then let software pick the line that makes the sum of squared errors (the red dashed gaps, squared and added up) as small as possible. That's least squares.

True: Y = a + bX + ea, b unknown; e = random error with mean zero
Estimated: Ŷ = â + b̂Xâ, b̂ = parameter estimates (the "hats" mean estimated from data)

Try it: TVs sold vs price at 10 Pittsburgh outlets

Drag the slope. The squared-error total bottoms out at b = −2.60. That's the line Excel reports: Q = 1631.47 − 2.60P. Read it as: each $1 price increase sells about 2.6 fewer TVs.
C1 Concept 3

Can you trust the number? t-stat, P-value, confidence interval

The coefficient is an estimate. A different sample would give a slightly different number. The standard error measures how much it would bounce around. Everything else is built from estimate ÷ SE.

Here's the TV price row from the Excel output, decoded:

Coefficient−2.60
Best guess of the slope
Std. error0.53
How wobbly the guess is
t-stat−4.89
= −2.60 ÷ 0.53. |t| ≥ 2 means significant
P-value0.0012
12-in-10,000 chance of a result this big if the true effect were 0
95% CI[−3.82, −1.37]
True slope is in here, 95% confident
t = b̂ / σb̂ 95% CI ≈ b̂ ± 2σb̂Rules of thumb. |t| > 2, P ≤ 0.05, or a CI that excludes zero all tell you the same thing: the effect is real (statistically different from 0). Rule-of-thumb CI for TV price: −2.60 ± 1.06 = [−3.66, −1.54], close to Excel's exact [−3.82, −1.37].

The fastest check: does the interval cross zero?

Each row is on its own scale; the vertical line is zero. If the bar crosses zero, you can't rule out "no effect."

R-square (0.75 for TVs) = share of the variation in Q the line explains. The F-test (Significance F) asks whether the model as a whole explains anything. Same reading: small = good.

D1 Concept 4

Curved data and multiple variables

Log-linear: straighten a curve

If the scatter curves, take the natural log of Y and X first, then run the same regression.

ln Y = β₀ + β₁ ln X + e

Multiple regression: more than one X

Same tool, more explanatory variables. Each coefficient is the effect of that variable holding the others constant. Need more observations than parameters.

Y = a + b₁X₁ + b₂X₂ + … + e
Same data, two views. Left: Q vs P curves. Right: ln Q vs ln P is a straight line, so ordinary regression works.
Caveat from the book: econometrics takes years to master (endogeneity, heteroskedasticity, etc.). Your job as a manager is to read and question the output, and lean on specialists to produce it.
E1 Concept 5

Elasticity: % response to a % change

An elasticity tells you how much demand moves, in percent, when something else moves 1 percent. It's unit-free, so you can compare across products.

EQ,G = %ΔQ / %ΔGG = whatever you change: own price, a rival's price, income, advertising
ElasticityChangesSign tells you
Own priceYour price|E| > 1 elastic raising price cuts revenue · |E| < 1 inelastic raising price raises revenue
Cross-pricePrice of good Y+ substitutes · − complements
IncomeConsumer income+ normal good · − inferior good
Own advertisingYour ad spend+ ads lift demand; size says how much
Cross-advertisingAd spend on good YEffect of a rival's/related ads on you

Real case: statin ads (Sinkinson & Starc, 2019)

Advertising elasticity is 0.32 for the top brand (Crestor) and 0.10 for #2 (Lipitor). For #2, a 10% ad increase lifts demand only 1%. Both are positive but under 1, so ads help, with diminishing bang for the buck.

F1 Concept 6

Elasticity from a linear demand equation

Q = α₀ + αXPX + αYPY + αMM + αHH
E = (coefficient) × (variable ÷ Q)Own price: E = αX · PX/Q. Cross: αY · PY/Q. Income: αM · M/Q.

Elasticity is not the slope. On a straight demand line the slope is constant, but elasticity changes as you move along it: inelastic at low prices, elastic at high prices, unit-elastic at the midpoint where revenue peaks.

Slide the price along the TV demand curve

Q = 1631.47 − 2.60P. At P = $400, E = −1.76; at P = $500, E = −3.92 (book's two examples). Revenue peaks near $314, where E = −1.
G1 Concept 7

Elasticity from log-linear demand: just read the coefficient

ln Q = β₀ + βX ln PX + βY ln PY + βM ln M + βH ln HEown = βX, Ecross = βY, Eincome = βM. Constant everywhere on the curve; doesn't depend on P or Q.
Linear demandElasticity = coefficient × variable ÷ Q.
Changes along the curve. Must plug in P and Q.
Log-linear demandElasticity = the coefficient itself.
Same at every point. No plugging in.

Real case: US gasoline (Hughes, Knittel & Sperling, 2008)

Log-linear estimates: price coefficient −0.04, income coefficient 0.53. A 10% price hike cuts quantity only 0.4% (very inelastic, so revenue rises). A 10% income rise lifts demand 5.3% (normal good).

Real case: King (Candy Crush) field experiment

King randomly varied gold-bar discounts across 14M+ players for three months. Infrequent buyers were more price-elastic than frequent buyers, so discounts aimed at infrequent buyers grow revenue more. Randomized prices are the cleanest way to get a demand curve you can trust.

Statin ad budget Concept 5

Top brand's ad elasticity is 0.32. How much must ads rise to lift demand 15%?

0.32 = 15% / %ΔA → %ΔA = 15 / 0.32 ≈ 47%.

Raincoats (log-linear) Concept 7

ln Q = … + 3 ln R − 2 ln AY. Effect of +10% rainfall? Of −10% ads on good Y?

Rain: 3 × 10% = +30%. Ads on Y: −2 × (−10%) = +20%. Good Y is a competitor, likely umbrellas.

Invigorated PED shoes (linear) Concept 6

QX = 100 − 3PX + 4PY − 0.01M + 2AX, with PX = 25, PY = 35, M = 20,000, AX = 50.

Q = 100 − 75 + 140 − 200 + 100 = 65.

  • Own: −3 × 25/65 = −1.15 → elastic; a price hike lowers revenue
  • Cross: 4 × 35/65 = 2.15 → substitutes
  • Income: −0.01 × 20,000/65 = −3.08 → inferior good
TV elasticity at two prices Concept 6

Q = 1631.47 − 2.60P. Find E at $400 and $500.

$400: Q = 591.5, E = −2.60 × 400/591.5 = −1.76. $500: Q = 331.5, E = −2.60 × 500/331.5 = −3.92. Higher price → more elastic.

Read a row: is it significant? Concept 3

Coefficient 0.54, SE 0.64. Significant?

t = 0.54/0.64 = 0.85. |t| < 2, and the CI [−1.02, 2.09] crosses zero → not significant. (This is FCI's advertising.)

Incremental call Concept 1

A project adds $50k revenue, $30k materials, and you'd keep paying your $40k manager either way. Do it?

Incremental: +50k − 30k = +$20k → yes. The manager's salary is unchanged, so ignore it.
